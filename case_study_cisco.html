The audit

The mess I walked into.

Cisco had just acquired Splunk, and the seams showed everywhere: inconsistent navigation, redundant content across domains, dead ends with no recovery path, and users unsure which brand they were even on. Leadership knew it was fragmented; what the team needed was where the friction lived and which of it mattered most.

I audited 8+ domains against Nielsen heuristics, watched Mouseflow recordings of real sessions, and mapped competitors, then turned it all into a prioritized, sprint-ready set of recommendations instead of a findings deck.

The audit, explorable. I ran the heuristic evaluation, then built this interactive impact × effort matrix with Claude Code so stakeholders could explore it themselves during the roadshow. I defined the rubric and every placement; the model handled the build.
01
Dead ends with no way back
Error and empty states stranded users: no recovery path, no next step. Flagged as high severity because every dead end was a potential lost evaluation.
02
Redundant content across domains
The same product story told three different ways on three different domains. Users couldn't tell which page was canonical.
03
Navigation that changed per surface
Cisco and Splunk surfaces used different wayfinding patterns, so learned behavior from one broke on the other.
04
Brand confusion at decision moments
At the exact points where users were deciding to trust the product, the branding told two different stories.
What happened next
I took the findings on a roadshow across the org with team-specific views, and six sprint teams moved recommendations into their planning. The audit stopped being documentation and became a shared reference. The highest-leverage artifact I made all summer wasn't a screen.
Findability, v1 · Shipped

Demos needed a front door.

Separate from the audit, I kept coming back to one problem in two forms: helping people find what they need. Version one was the Demo Center. Splunk's demos were scattered across the site. Useful, but buried. I designed the Demo Center as the single front door: one IA, one component strategy, one copy hierarchy.

Before / after. From a five-step filter maze under Resources to a two-step front door.

Iterations

Filtering felt like the obvious answer: the old site was a filter maze, and users asked for filters. So I explored a bunch of placements to get them right.

Stakeholder review and 8 user tests narrowed it to one direction.

What testing taught me

01
Give people somewhere to start.
Users gravitated toward the featured demo as an easy starting point, so I kept featured content prominent instead of presenting every demo with equal weight.
02
Time matters before the click.
Users wanted to know a demo's duration before committing, so I surfaced time alongside each demo to make the choice easier.
03
Categories made a large catalog feel smaller.
Users responded well to browsing by Security, Observability, and other product areas, validating the pre-filtered categories as a faster path to relevant demos.
TRADEOFFS

Not every user request made it into the final design.

Testing gave me clear signals, but the final experience also had to account for product constraints, content scale, and business priorities.

01 REMOVED

Duration

Users wanted Time before committing

Users asked how long demos would take, but stakeholders confirmed they were self-paced with no reliable duration. Rather than introduce a misleading number, I removed time from the final design.

02 SIMPLIFIED

Filters

I explored More ways to narrow

With a relatively small demo library, persistent filters added more interface than value. Users already responded well to pre-categorized paths like Security and Observability, so I removed the extra filtering layer and let categories do the work.

03 ADVOCATED FOR

Featured demo

Business questioned Giving one demo priority

Stakeholders initially didn't want a featured demo, but testing showed that users valued having a clear place to start. I advocated for keeping it prominent, using the research to support the decision.

Findability, v2 · Hackathon → proposal

The copy button was a beginning, not an end.

Version two started smaller: developers landed on technical blogs, copied a code snippet, and left. Everyone treated copy as the end of the journey. I treated it as a high-intent signal and explored it through an org hackathon.

BeforeOld snippet UI: weak hierarchy, icon-only copy
AfterYour redesigned snippet component

Old vs. new: the same component, rethought: clearer hierarchy, explicit "Copy," built to be maintained.

I started from a question: what if this is a door deeper into the ecosystem, uniquely for each user?

The idea: surface the right resources from each user's activity plus the snippet's context: the tutorial, doc, or guide that matches what they just grabbed, not a generic related-links list. If the Demo Center was findability at the site level, this was findability at the moment level.

The concept: copy a snippet, get an AI-curated next step.

Pressure-testing with SMEs

Before writing the proposal, I brought the concept to subject-matter experts to find out what the backend would actually have to do.

01
What signals could power personalization?
Explored how snippet interactions, site behavior, and user context could signal intent and help surface more relevant resources.
02
What would the AI require behind the scenes?
Worked with AI and engineering SMEs to understand the APIs, storage, latency, and backend load required to generate recommendations dynamically.
03
What was realistic to build first?
Separated the vision from the MVP: start with contextual resources tied to the snippet, then evolve toward behavior-aware personalization as the infrastructure matured.
The call
Deprioritized the personalization layer. The SME conversations made the math clear: tracking user history across the backend was heavy effort for an uncertain payoff, while the snippet redesign itself was a quick win. So I scoped the work to the component, documented the full concept with SME findings and specs, and left it with the team when my internship ended.
Concept testing
All six participants said they'd want this. One change came out of it: making copy explicit with the word "Copy" instead of an icon alone.
Visual specs: anatomy, type scale, and spacing documented for engineering handoff.
Interaction specs: every state (default, hover, copied, AI-suggestion) defined so behavior is never ambiguous in build.
What I'd refine now
I was newer to designing for AI trust then. If I continued today, I'd make the reasoning behind each recommendation clearer, add stronger user controls, and test how much personalization developers actually want inside a technical workflow.
Reflection

What I'd do differently.

This internship taught me how to create momentum inside a large organization, including teaching a Figma Make workshop for the team on the AI-assisted workflow I'd been using all summer. Three things stuck with me:

01
Clarity compounds.
The highest-leverage work I did wasn't a screen, it was making the system legible. A shared audit six teams could plan from. One front door for every demo. I'd look for that kind of leverage even earlier next time, instead of waiting to stumble into it.
02
Evidence moves decisions.
Mouseflow recordings, usability tests, and competitor patterns gave me stronger footing than any opinion. When I could show a user struggling, the debate ended.
03
Name the tool, own the judgment.
Claude Code built my audit matrix; Figma Make accelerated explorations. The tools gave me speed, but every rubric, placement, and final call was mine. I'd be even more explicit about that split going forward: show the AI's output, then show where I overrode it.
