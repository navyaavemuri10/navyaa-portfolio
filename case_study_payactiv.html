The problem

Admins have learned to work around the tool instead of with it.

New admins need about 30 hours of training to use Core. Experienced admins know it so well that its friction has become invisible to them.

A moment that stuck with me

Admins knew exactly how to get to what they needed. But when I asked a slight variation of a task, the kind that will come up with the new app product we’re launching, they hesitated and didn’t know what to do.

The solution

Two key ideas reorganize Core around how admins actually work.

The goal: help any admin, new or experienced, get straight to their task and know exactly what they’re changing, without 30 hours of training.

01

Core is organized around what admins are trying to do, so every task has an obvious place to start.

I got buy-in from support, ops, compliance, finance, engineering, design, and product through design reviews. I came with a clear point of view and my rationale, and invited everyone to poke holes. One question changed the design.

The hole they poked

“If a business inherits everything from its partner, why does setting up a product for it need a full setup process?”

What I changed

Businesses can change anything their partner sets. So setup now starts in draft mode, shows which values are pre-filled from the partner, and makes clear which ones an admin can change for that business.

SoMapping it together

I was brand new to Core, and the tool was so clunky there was no way I could have mapped it on my own. With guidance from my manager and Core’s PM, who’s close to a subject matter expert, we put our brains together to build an experience map of every dependency.

How it played out

The two ideas, applied to real admin work.

Support is time-sensitive, so lookup comes first.

Support specialists are helping someone in real time. I split support into user lookup and business lookup and put both at the front of Core.

1User lookup

Business lookup sits right beside it. 2 clicks when searching for a specific user.

PrincipleChunkingTwo lookups that match what admins actually do.

One home for partner configurations.

Partner configurations are where an admin sets up the values every business under a partner inherits. Before, each partner had its own spot in the side nav, which got really clunky. Since it’s usually one person making all of these changes, I gave them one dedicated section.

Three layouts I explored for each partner's settings

Cards (chosen): easy to compare and discover.

State table: good for comparing, but adds a step.

Requirement table: direct, but too dense too early.

I went with cards. Each product, like EWA and Paycard, gets its own card, so admins can compare them at a glance and find what they need without digging.

Confirming changes before they move money.

Admins see the new value next to the saved one, with the scope of the override, before anything goes live.

Editing a state override in a side drawer, without losing your place. I prototyped the motion with ChatGPT from my specs.

What this process taught me

Start with the system, and get the foundation right.

I came in with a few configuration stories, but the real work was underneath them. Once the foundation was right, every page after it, from support lookups to partner configurations, had an obvious place to go.
