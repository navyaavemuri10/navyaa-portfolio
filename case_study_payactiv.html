Redesigning Payactiv’s back office so new admins can learn it in hours, not weeks

I led the redesign of Core’s information architecture, the back office tool behind everything on the Payactiv app.

The problem

Admins have learned to work around the tool instead of with it.

New admins need about 30 hours of training to use Core. Experienced admins know it so well that its friction has become invisible to them.

Core is the back office behind every layer

The solution

Two key ideas reorganize Core around how admins actually work.

The goal: help any admin, new or experienced, get straight to their task and know exactly what they’re changing, without 30 hours of training.

01

Intent-based navigation

Core is organized around what admins are trying to do, so every task has an obvious place to start.

What the admin wants to doWhere it lives in Core
“Help a user who called in”User lookup
“Check on a client business”Business lookup
“Change a partner’s settings”Configurations
“See how a product is doing”Reports & Analytics

02

Product at the center

Every task starts by choosing a product, so admins only see what applies to it.

I got buy-in from every stakeholder group through design reviews. I explained my rationale and came with a clear point of view, but also invited them to poke holes, which made the navigation stronger.

How I got there

I learned the domain fast, then mapped it with the people who knew it best.

FirstGetting up to speed

I used ChatGPT to get up to speed on fintech concepts, so I could bring better questions to my team. When I hit something unfamiliar, I had it break the concept down, then talked it through with my PM and team to make sure I had it right.

SoMapping it together

I was brand new to Core, and the tool was so clunky there was no way I could have mapped it on my own. With guidance from my manager and Core’s PM, who’s close to a subject matter expert, we put our brains together to build an experience map of every dependency.

Experience mapCropped view of the FigJam map, with two or three callouts on the dependencies that mattered most

Once we could see it all, two things stood out: everything depends on product, and settings cascade down.

Design principles

I used AI to explore ideas, and design fundamentals to make decisions.

Chunking

Breaking a dense system into pieces admins can take in at a glance.

Preventing errors

Catching mistakes before they move money.

Designing to scale

Building patterns that hold up as Core grows.

How it played out

The two ideas, applied to real admin work.

Support is time-sensitive, so lookup comes first.

Support specialists are helping someone in real time. I split support into user lookup and business lookup and put both at the front of Core.

One home for partner configurations.

Partner configurations are where an admin sets up the values every business under a partner inherits. Before, each partner had its own spot in the side nav, which got really clunky. Since it’s usually one person making all of these changes, I gave them one dedicated section.

The details

Small details, built for speed and trust.

Status at a glance. Icon plus label on every pill.

Finding things fast. Search plus State and Status filters.

No access: restricted sections leave the nav, and direct links explain why.

View-only: same structure, and Close replaces Save changes.

No single layout fits every page, so the content decides.

I explored three ways to show requirements, and realized they didn’t all have to fit into one box. The layout adapts: cards when there are only a couple of products, like EWA and Paycard, and tables when admins need to compare across many states.

Cards: simple and easy to discover.

State table: built for comparing.

Requirement table: direct, but dense too early.
